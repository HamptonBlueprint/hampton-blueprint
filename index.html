People Infrastructure Sprint

Rebuilding Structure After Rapid Growth

How Hampton Blueprint redesigned a 15-person company that had outgrown its original structure in 18 months.

Client
Youth Mentorship Non-Profit
Industry
Non-Profit/Civic Engagement
Team Size
15 Employees

People Infrastructure Sprint

The Challenge

A local non-profit with a focus on youth mentorship grew from 2 to 15 employees in under 18 months — faster than anyone anticipated. What started as a lean, founder-led operation had become a 15-person team with no formal structure, no defined management layer, and accountability gaps that were costing the business real money and real talent.

The CEO was carrying 6 direct reports. Decisions stalled daily waiting for her approval. Three key employees had given informal notice within two months — not because of compensation, but because they did not understand their role or their future at the company. Sales and operations were in open conflict over ownership of client relationships. The org chart, when finally drawn, revealed seven different people with overlapping responsibilities for the same functions.

"We hired great people and then put them in a broken structure. The problem was never the team — it was that we never took the time to design how the team was supposed to work."
— CEO, Youth Mentorship Non-Profit

The Strategic Approach

Hampton Blueprint entered the engagement with a full discovery and assessment before making a single recommendation. Over three weeks, we conducted structured leadership interviews, met individually with key team members across departments, reviewed the informal reporting structure, and mapped every significant decision to understand where authority actually lived versus where it was supposed to live.

The discovery revealed three distinct structural problems: an oversized span of control at the top, undefined department boundaries between fundraising efforts and operations, and the absence of any mid-level management accountability. No amount of hiring or team-building would solve these problems. They required structural redesign.

In Phase 2, we designed a three-tier organizational structure appropriate for a non-profit of our client's current size and projected 18-month growth. This included:

  • A redesigned org chart reducing CEO direct reports from 6 to 2
  • Two formally created department structures with named leads and documented authority
  • Role clarity documentation for all 8 key positions
  • A RACI matrix covering 14 recurring business-critical decisions
  • A 30/60/90 onboarding framework applied to two planned hires
  • Three core HR policies drafted and delivered

The final 90-day roadmap prioritized implementation by urgency — which structural changes to make in the first 30 days, which required team communication and change management in days 31–60, and which required new processes in the final phase.

The Outcome

Within 60 days of final delivery, the non-profit had implemented the new structure and launched the first two management conversations using the accountability framework we built. The CEO's direct report load dropped from 6 to 2 within the first month of implementation. Both pending resignations were retained following direct conversations enabled by the clarity the new structure created.

With increased ability to expand it's reach within the community, this client has since engaged Hampton Blueprint on a Tier 2 monthly retainer to continue evolving their people infrastructure through their next growth phase.

The Challenge

One of our consulting agency clients had a recurring problem that no one could name precisely. Things kept falling through the cracks — client commitments missed, internal projects stalled, decisions made by one person that another person thought they owned. The COO described it as "a company where everyone is responsible and no one is accountable."

With 22 employees and no formal HR function, the agency did not know what was structurally broken. They only knew that the dysfunction was getting more expensive as the team grew.

"We didn't need someone to fix everything immediately. We needed someone to tell us exactly what was broken — so we could fix the right things in the right order."
— COO, Consulting Agency Client

The Strategic Approach

We entered the engagement with the Organizational Assessment — a focused 30-day diagnostic designed to map reality before proposing solutions. Through structured interviews with the COO and four key team members, a review of the informal role structure, and an accountability mapping exercise, we built a clear picture of where organizational dysfunction was concentrated.

The assessment revealed that 60% of recurring dysfunction traced to two root causes: four overlapping roles with undefined ownership boundaries, and an absence of any documented decision authority for the eight most consequential recurring decisions the company made weekly. These were not people problems. They were design problems.

We delivered a 28-page written assessment report with findings organized by urgency and a prioritized set of 12 recommendations — including which three changes, if made immediately, would produce the fastest reduction in day-to-day friction.

The Outcome

Our client implemented the top three priority recommendations within 30 days of receiving the report. Within 60 days, they engaged Hampton Blueprint for the full People Infrastructure Sprint to address the remaining structural redesign. They have since moved to a Tier 1 monthly retainer for ongoing people operations support.

The Challenge

Our restaurant client, extremely new to the industry, was at 21 employees, growing at approximately 25% per week, with zero documented people infrastructure. No onboarding program. No written job descriptions. No performance management process. No HR policies beyond what the owner had verbally communicated over the years.

The owner knew the risk — one bad hire, one performance dispute, one employee departure who took institutional knowledge with them — and the business would have no foundation to recover from. But she did not know where to start, and the business was too busy growing to slow down long enough to figure it out.

"We were building the plane while flying it. Hampton Blueprint helped us land long enough to actually blueprint the thing."
— Owner, Restaurant Client

The Strategic Approach

We recommended the full People Infrastructure Sprint for their establishment — not because they had a structural crisis, but because they were about to. A company growing at 25% per week without people infrastructure is a company building structural debt at an accelerating rate.

The Sprint delivered a complete build from scratch: a first-ever organizational chart designed for their projected 60-person team, role profiles for all 10 current key positions, a 30/60/90 onboarding program, a performance review framework, an employee handbook covering five core policies, and a 90-day implementation roadmap prioritized by what would create the most value fastest.

Unlike many Sprint engagements where the primary challenge is redesigning a broken structure, this particular client required building a structure from first principles. This meant deeper discovery conversations to understand the informal operating model that had developed organically, and more careful design work to ensure the new structure reflected how the business actually made decisions.

The Outcome

Our restaurant client successfully launched their first formal onboarding program within 45 days of final delivery. Thirty-three new hires went through it within the first 60 days, with the owner describing the difference as "night and day" compared to previous onboarding experiences.

Recognizing that people infrastructure requires ongoing attention as the company grows, our restaurant engaged Hampton Blueprint on a Tier 2 Growth Retainer to continue evolving the foundation through their next growth phase.

Why Your Org Chart Isn't the Problem (But It's Definitely a Symptom)

January 15, 2026 · Hampton Blueprint

When a small business owner tells us their company is chaotic, our first question is always the same: how many direct reports does leadership carry? Nine times out of ten, the answer reveals the real problem. It is not the people. It is not the strategy. It is the structure.

"Most small business owners think their chaos is a hiring problem. It is not. It is a structure problem — and the good news is that structure is fixable."

What Your Org Chart Is Actually Telling You

An org chart is a map — not of how you want things to work, but of how decisions actually get made, where accountability lives, and what path problems travel before they get resolved. When that map is missing or outdated, it means the organization has been growing without a design intention behind it.

Most small businesses reach 20–30 employees before anyone draws their first honest org chart. By that point, the structure that exists is not the one anyone chose — it is the one that accumulated.

Three Signs Your Structure Is the Real Problem

  • Decisions stall at the top — if everything requires your approval, your structure does not distribute authority effectively.
  • Things fall through the cracks — not because your team is irresponsible, but because no one was clearly responsible.
  • Adding people creates more chaos — new hires should reduce chaos. If they are adding to it, the structure they are entering is broken.

The Org Chart Is Not the Fix — It Is the Starting Point

When we begin an engagement with a client, we do not start by drawing a new org chart. We start by understanding the current reality. The org chart comes later — after we understand the gaps. It is the output of clear thinking, not the input to it.

The 90-Day Onboarding Framework Every Growing Company Needs

February 3, 2026 · Hampton Blueprint

Most small businesses have an onboarding program that lasts about three days. Day one is paperwork. Day two is a team tour. Day three is "here's your login — figure it out." Then they wonder why their new hire is still asking basic questions at week six.

"The cost of poor onboarding is not visible on any invoice. It shows up in productivity loss, early attrition, and managers spending their best hours explaining things that should have been documented."

Why 30 Days Is Not Enough

Employees who experience a structured 90-day onboarding process are significantly more likely to remain at the company for three or more years. Yet most small business onboarding ends before the employee fully understands their role, the culture, or what success looks like.

The Three-Phase Framework We Build

  • Days 1–30: Learn. Focus on orientation, systems access, and understanding the company's mission and how the employee's role contributes to it. Weekly check-ins with their manager.
  • Days 31–60: Contribute. The employee begins taking ownership of core responsibilities with decreasing supervision. Midpoint review conversation.
  • Days 61–90: Lead. The employee is functioning independently and beginning to identify improvements. Final 90-day review.

This framework is one of the core deliverables we build in every People Infrastructure Sprint engagement. If you do not have it yet, start there.

From 10 to 50 Employees: The Structural Inflection Points You Can't Ignore

March 5, 2026 · Hampton Blueprint

Every company that grows from 10 to 50 employees passes through predictable structural inflection points — moments where the way things work stops being sufficient. The businesses that navigate these transitions deliberately come out stronger. The ones that do not often plateau, fracture, or lose key people.

The 15-Employee Inflection Point

At around 15 employees, informal communication stops working. When a business has 10 people, information travels through conversations. At 15, things start to fall through the cracks. This is when you need your first documented processes, clear role definitions, and a regular team communication cadence.

The 25-Employee Inflection Point

At 25 employees, a formal management layer becomes necessary. The business owner can no longer effectively supervise everyone. This is when the company needs its first true managers with clear authority and accountability.

The 40-Employee Inflection Point

At 40 employees, departments emerge. This is when org design, RACI frameworks, and people operations infrastructure stop being nice-to-haves and become operational necessities.

"The businesses that struggle most at each inflection point are not the ones that grew too fast. They are the ones that grew without updating the operating system underneath the growth."

What a RACI Matrix Actually Does (And Why Your Team Needs One)

March 20, 2026 · Hampton Blueprint

RACI stands for Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. It is a simple framework that answers one of the most disruptive questions in any growing company: who owns this decision?

Breaking Down the Four Roles

  • Responsible: The person who does the work. There should usually be exactly one person responsible for each decision.
  • Accountable: The person who owns the outcome — answerable if it does not happen.
  • Consulted: People whose input should be sought before the decision is made.
  • Informed: People who need to know what was decided, but who do not influence the decision.

The Most Common Mistake

The most common mistake small businesses make is having too many people in the Responsible column. When two people are both responsible for the same outcome, neither truly is. Clarity requires naming one person — and that naming often requires uncomfortable conversations.

A RACI matrix is one of the core deliverables we build in every Sprint engagement. It is not a corporate bureaucracy tool — it is a communication and accountability tool that pays for itself in the first month of use.

Five HR Policies Every Small Business Needs Before Employee #10

April 1, 2026 · Hampton Blueprint

You do not need a comprehensive employee handbook before your first hire. But you do need a handful of foundational policies before your team reaches ten people — because that is when informal agreements start creating real legal and operational risk.

"Most small businesses do not think about HR policies until there is a problem. The goal is to have them before the problem creates the need."

The Five Policies

1. PTO and Leave Policy

How does paid time off work? How much do employees accrue, when can they use it, and what happens to unused time? Without a written policy, you will answer this question differently every time someone asks.

2. Harassment and Discrimination Policy

Federal law requires that employees understand their rights and your company's commitment to a harassment-free workplace. This policy must exist, be distributed, and be acknowledged in writing.

3. Disciplinary Process

When something goes wrong, how do you handle it? A written progressive disciplinary process protects the company legally and gives managers a consistent path to follow.

4. Remote Work or Workplace Expectations Policy

Whether in-person, remote, or hybrid — documenting expectations around hours, availability, and communication prevents ambiguity before it becomes conflict.

5. Confidentiality and Data Protection Policy

A basic confidentiality policy — reinforced with an acknowledgment signature — protects your business in the event of a departure or dispute.

These five policies are core deliverables in every People Infrastructure Sprint. If you do not have them yet, a discovery call is a good place to start.
